Abstract

Closed-loop driving simulators populate their environments with non-ego traffic agents that behave much the same way, whether from rule-based traffic managers or from learned models trained toward a single behavioral mode. Recent work introduces style variation through post-hoc labels or LLM-inferred reward weights, but these act as proxies for what a style should reward rather than demonstrations of humans explicitly asked to drive in that style.

We introduce PersonaDrive, a pipeline that conditions a vision-language-action (VLA) driving agent on retrieved demonstrations from a style-instructed dataset in which 15 participants drive CARLA Leaderboard routes under aggressive, neutral, and conservative instructions on a driver-in-the-loop rig. The pipeline mines behavioral triplets offline with a combined image-text similarity score, trains a lightweight retrieval head fusing frozen visual features with a small control encoder over per-style databases, and fine-tunes one VLA backbone to read retrieved context points as in-context demonstrations. Switching style at inference is a single database swap, so one set of weights serves every style with no per-style retraining.

On Bench2Drive, PersonaDrive (no style) improves the driving score by 4.6% over SimLingo and 2.5% over HiP-AD. Under style conditioning it attains the highest driving score in every style within a 2.1-point band (87.55–89.62), its weakest style surpassing the strongest baseline (DMW) by 5.8%, while average speed and acceleration rise by 19.8% and 24.9% from the conservative to the aggressive instruction. Each agent is evaluated individually as a driving policy.

Why Human-Style Traffic Agents?

What is missing in simulated traffic?
Rule-based traffic managers and learned traffic models produce agents that behave alike. Real traffic spans conservative, neutral and aggressive drivers, and those differences matter most at merges, unprotected turns and in dense urban traffic, where the ego policy has to react to the people around it.

Why not style labels or reward weights?
Existing personalization methods supervise style with post-hoc observational labels or with reward weights inferred by an LLM. Both approximate what a style should reward. Neither records what people actually do when they are asked to drive in that style.

What does PersonaDrive do instead?
We recorded people driving under explicit style instructions and store their frames in one database per style. At every tick, a retrieval head finds the most similar context points in the selected database, and the VLA backbone reads them as in-context demonstrations before predicting its own waypoints.

Contributions

  1. A controlled style driving dataset. 15 drivers drive CARLA Leaderboard routes under aggressive, neutral and conservative instructions on a driver-in-the-loop rig. The design is paired at the route level, which decouples style from driver identity (29,777 frames, 55 routes, 21 scenario types).
  2. A style-conditioned triplet-RAG architecture. We mine behavioral triplets (Stage 1), train a retrieval head over per-style databases (Stage 2), and fine-tune the VLA backbone to read retrieved context points as in-context demonstrations (Stage 3). Style control comes from retrieved human demonstrations, not from style tokens, proxy rewards or per-style fine-tuning.
  3. Closed-loop results on Bench2Drive. Without style, PersonaDrive improves the Driving Score by 4.6% over SimLingo (88.95 vs. 85.07) and 2.5% over HiP-AD (86.77). With style conditioning it has the highest Driving Score in every style, its weakest style still 5.8% above the strongest baseline (DMW), while speed and acceleration rise 19.8% and 24.9% from the conservative to the aggressive instruction with no per-style retraining. A retrieval-depth ablation shows open-loop and closed-loop accuracy moving together with the number of retrieved demonstrations on a fixed checkpoint.

Style-Instructed Driving Dataset

Data comes from 15 non-professional drivers with valid licenses on a driver-in-the-loop rig: a Next Level Racing GTrack seat, three 50-inch monitors covering about 130° and a Logitech G923 wheel and pedals. CARLA runs in synchronous mode at 20 Hz. The ego vehicle is the only human-controlled actor and TrafficManager drives the rest of the traffic. Each participant drives the routes in randomized order, once under each instruction.

Method

1 Triplet mining

Each frame is written out as a frame sentence (speed, steering, command, VQA annotation, commentary, GPS target and the executed waypoints). A combined score of SigLIP image similarity and BGE-M3 sentence similarity selects positives, easy negatives and hard negatives: frames that look alike but where the driver used a different command or speed.

2 Retrieval head

Frozen SigLIP features (768-d) are concatenated with a trained ControlEncoder over speed, throttle, steering and command (128-d) and projected to a 1024-d embedding. A weighted triplet loss trains it, with extra weight on hard negatives. One head serves all styles; only the queried index changes.

3 In-context SFT

The VLA backbone (InternVL2-1B, inherited from SimLingo) is fine-tuned with a rank-16 LoRA to read the K = 2 retrieved context points as few-shot examples. It receives no style label and has to infer the style from the demonstrations it is given.
